# The Jordaan Was Never Supposed to Be This Beautiful

Most people see the Jordaan on foot. Wrong way round, if you ask us.

Here's the thing about this neighborhood: it was never actually designed. One canal over, the grand ring — Prinsengracht, Herengracht, Keizersgracht — was drawn up on purpose, a showcase for Amsterdam's merchant class, geometric and deliberate. The Jordaan wasn't. It went up fast and cheap in the early 1600s, a project the city called Het Nieuwe Werck — the New Work — to house the working class and the immigrants nobody was designing anything nice for. Nobody even bothered with a grid. The streets and canals just follow whatever drainage ditches and footpaths were already sitting on the land, which is the actual reason they meet the Prinsengracht at odd angles instead of straight ones.

Four hundred years later, that's still basically the whole story: this is a neighborhood built to be lived in, not admired, and somehow that's exactly why people love it more than the ring next door.

Nobody's fully sure where the name Jordaan comes from, either. The popular theory says it's from the French *jardin* — garden — because half the streets and canals here are named after flowers and trees. There's an older theory too, that locals started calling the Prinsengracht "the river Jordan" and the name just stuck to the whole neighborhood. Historians still argue about which one's true. We like that nobody's settled it.

Glide in from the Brouwersgracht — the Jordaan's northern edge, and what used to be Brewers' Canal, named in 1594 for the breweries that once lined it — and the first things you pass are already making the point. Right on the corner where the Prinsengracht meets the Brouwersgracht sits a squat 1641 step-gabled house, solid and unfussy. A few doors down, three gable stones — carved around 1620 by a builder named Egbert Daelder, nobody famous — show three saints: Peter, Paul, and Paul again on the road to Emmaus. Nobody commissioned a monument. Somebody just wanted a good story on their own front wall.

The Noorderkerk holds down Noordermarkt square just past it, built fast in the 1620s to keep pace with how quickly the Jordaan itself was filling up — a church shaped like a Greek cross wrapped in an octagon, which is a strange, practical way to fit a lot of people in a hurry.

Keep heading south and you'll cross the Bloemgracht on the Kees de Jongenbrug — a bridge that, tellingly, isn't named after an admiral or a regent. It's named after a fictional Amsterdam boy from a Theo Thijssen novel, at the request of the small museum that keeps his memory. That's about the size of who this neighborhood tends to honor.

Duck up the Bloemgracht itself and the flower theme takes over completely — this is the Flower Canal, and the Egelantiersgracht next to it is named for the eglantine rose. On the Bloemgracht, keep an eye on numbers 87 through 91: three stepped-gable houses from 1642, De Drie Hendricken, each with a stone showing who used to live there — a farmer, a townsman, a sailor. Just off the Egelantiersgracht, the Sint Andrieshofje hides behind an easy-to-miss entrance — a quiet courtyard almshouse said to date to 1614, built to house widows who needed somewhere to land. You'd walk straight past it if you didn't know to look.

Back on the Prinsengracht, Zon's Hofje picks up the same thread at number 159 — another almshouse, this one built in 1765 for elderly Mennonite women, plain and dignified behind its own gate. A little further on, at 211 through 217, four identical 17th-century warehouses stand shoulder to shoulder — Pakhuis de Arend, built around 1690 with matching spout gables and not one ounce of decoration beyond what the job needed. They were built to store goods, not to impress anyone walking past.

This stretch does a lot of the talking. The Anne Frank House sits right on the water around number 263, beside the Westerkerk, whose tower has quietly worn a replica of the Imperial Crown of Austria since Maximilian I granted Amsterdam the right to use it, back in 1489. Rembrandt is buried somewhere inside that church — nobody knows exactly where. He died with almost nothing, and in the 1600s a poor man's grave didn't come with a name plate. His memorial stone wasn't added until 1909, two and a half centuries later. Even Amsterdam's most famous painter didn't get special treatment here.

Slip around the back of the Westerkerk onto the Keizersgracht and you'll find the Homomonument — three plain pink granite triangles, unveiled in 1987, the first memorial anywhere in the world for gay men and lesbians persecuted under the Nazis. They're not called anything grander than that. The monument's own inscription, carved right into the stone, says simply that the three triangles stand for past, present, and future: one at street level carries a line from a 1917 poem and faces the Anne Frank House; one steps down into the canal itself and faces the National Monument on Dam Square; one sits raised, a small podium, facing where the Dutch gay-rights organization COC once had its headquarters. You can put your hand in the water at the middle one. People do.

Further south, moored near the Elandsgracht around number 296, is the Houseboat Museum — a genuine 1914 cargo ship, rebuilt into somebody's actual home, opened to strangers in 1997 by the man who used to just live there. Just past it, at number 300, De Rode Vos wears a carved fox with a bird in its beak over the door — folk iconography, not a coat of arms, put there by an ordinary merchant family and rebuilt in 1767.

Follow the water all the way down to where the Prinsengracht meets the Leidsegracht — the Jordaan's southern edge — and the bridge there closes the loop nicely. It's called the Angenietje Swarthofbrug, officially, since 2021, after a woman who spent years as a foster-nurse for the city's orphanage and cared for 126 abandoned children. Not an aristocrat. Not an admiral. Just someone who showed up for kids nobody else wanted.

This is still a neighborhood that sings, too — literally. The Jordaanlied, the city's own genre of nostalgic, slightly weepy folk song, was born here and peaked in the 1950s, before finding a second life in the '70s. It came from people who mostly had each other and not much else.

None of this was built to be admired. It was built to be used, lived in, sung about by people who needed somewhere to be from. Maybe that's still the whole difference.
